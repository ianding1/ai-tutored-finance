The investment portfolio is only one part of a household's finances. Before deciding on a stock share, look at all of the household's assets and liabilities, and at the risks that the portfolio cannot, and should not, carry.

## The household balance sheet [#balance-sheet]

The <Def id="household-balance-sheet">household balance sheet</Def> lists a household's assets and liabilities: financial assets (cash, taxable accounts, retirement accounts), real assets such as a home, and liabilities such as the mortgage, car loans, student loans and credit cards; the difference is net worth.

For a household of working age, the largest asset is usually not in any account but is human capital (<Ref concept="human-capital" />): the present value of future earnings. Human capital cannot be sold or diversified; its risk depends on how secure the job is and how closely earnings move with the stock market and with the employer's stock (<Ref lesson="4.12" sec="human-capital" />). Many household financial decisions are really about protecting or supplementing human capital.

## The emergency fund [#emergency-fund]

An <Def id="emergency-fund">emergency fund</Def> is money set aside for job loss, illness or unexpected expenses, available at any time and stable in value, usually held in a high-yield savings account, a money market fund or short-term Treasuries. A common guideline is 3 to 6 months of essential expenses; households with unsteady income, a single earner or high layoff risk in their industry need more.

The emergency fund lets a household avoid selling investments at a bad time or borrowing on high-rate credit cards when something goes wrong. Its return is low, which can be seen as the insurance premium for this protection.

## Managing debt [#debt]

Interest rates on debts differ widely. Credit card rates are often above 20%; almost no investment can reliably beat that, so such debt should be paid off first. For a lower-rate mortgage, a common question is whether spare money should go to prepaying the mortgage or to investing.

Prepaying 1 dollar of the mortgage saves the interest on that dollar from then on, which amounts to a return equal to the mortgage rate, with no risk. If mortgage interest is not itemized (the 2026 standard deduction for married couples filing jointly is 32,200 dollars, and many households no longer itemize), this is also the after-tax return (<Ref check="Q1" />).

## Insurance [#insurance]

Insurance swaps a possible large loss for a certain small payment. For a risk-averse person this can be worthwhile even when the premium exceeds the expected loss (<Ref lesson="1.4" sec="insurance" />). But insurers have costs and need a profit, so premiums are on average higher than expected payouts; insurance therefore suits rare but large losses that one could not afford, while small losses are better borne oneself, for example by choosing higher deductibles.

The kinds of insurance households most often need:

- <Def id="term-life-insurance">Term life insurance</Def>: if the insured person dies within the agreed term (say 20 years), the insurer pays the face amount. It protects the part of human capital the family depends on, at a premium far below that of whole life insurance, which combines insurance with savings.
- <Def id="disability-insurance">Disability insurance</Def>: if illness or injury prevents work, it pays part of the income each month, usually around 60%. The US Social Security Administration estimates that more than one in four of today's 20-year-olds will become disabled before reaching retirement age.
- Umbrella insurance adds, on top of the liability limits of car and home insurance, further liability coverage of, for example, 1 million dollars, protecting against losing most of one's property in a lawsuit.
- Health insurance, home and car insurance, and insurance for long-term care.

## Estate planning [#estate]

Estate planning decides who receives property and decision-making power when a family member dies or can no longer act:

- A will names how property is divided and who becomes guardian of minor children.
- A <Def id="beneficiary-designation">beneficiary designation</Def>: retirement accounts, life insurance and similar assets can name beneficiaries directly; on the owner's death they pass straight to the beneficiaries without going through probate.
- A revocable living trust: assets are placed in a trust with oneself as trustee; on death they are distributed according to the trust document, also without probate and without being made public.
- Powers of attorney and health care directives name who handles finances and makes medical decisions if one cannot.

Federal estate tax concerns only very large estates: under the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the exemption is 15 million dollars per person in 2026, adjusted for inflation afterwards; some states have their own estate taxes with much lower thresholds.

## Summary [#summary]

- The household balance sheet includes financial assets, real assets, liabilities, and the largest asset, human capital.
- The emergency fund holds 3 to 6 months or more of essential expenses, somewhere stable and always available.
- Pay off high-rate debt first; prepaying a mortgage earns a riskless return equal to the mortgage rate.
- Insurance suits rare but large losses; term life and disability insurance protect human capital, and umbrella insurance protects property from liability claims.
- Wills, beneficiary designations, trusts and powers of attorney decide where property and decisions go.
