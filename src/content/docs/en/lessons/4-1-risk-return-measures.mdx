## Two average returns [#averages]

An investment returned $R_1, \ldots, R_n$ over the past $n$ years (<Ref concept="simple-return" />). There are two ways to compute an "average return".

The <Def id="arithmetic-mean-return">arithmetic mean return</Def> is the simple average of the yearly returns:

$$
\bar R = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} R_t
$$

The <Def id="geometric-mean-return">geometric mean return</Def> is the yearly rate that, compounded, gives the same final wealth; it is also called the compound annual growth rate:

$$
G = \left[(1 + R_1)(1 + R_2) \cdots (1 + R_n)\right]^{1/n} - 1
$$

For example, a fund rises 25% in the first year and falls 20% in the second. The arithmetic mean is 2.5%, but $1.25 \times 0.8 = 1$: after two years wealth has not changed, so the geometric mean is 0.

The two answer different questions:

- The arithmetic mean estimates the expected return for next year (<Ref concept="expected-value" />). If each year's return comes from the same distribution, it is an unbiased estimate of the expected return.
- The geometric mean describes the growth the holder actually got over the period. In terms of the <Ref concept="log-return" />, $\ln(1 + G)$ is exactly the average of the yearly log returns.

Unless all yearly returns are equal, the geometric mean is below the arithmetic mean. In <Ref lesson="0.4" sec="mean-median" />, median wealth was below mean wealth; that is the other side of the same fact: long-run median wealth grows roughly at the geometric mean, while mean wealth grows at the arithmetic mean.

## Volatility drag [#volatility-drag]

How far below the arithmetic mean is the geometric mean? Let each year's return have expected value $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. One can show (<Ref check="Q1" />) that

$$
G \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}
$$

Volatility pushes the compound growth rate below the arithmetic mean; this is <Def id="volatility-drag">volatility drag</Def>. For example, with $\mu = 10\%$ and $\sigma = 20\%$, $G \approx 10\% - \frac{(20\%)^2}{2} = 8\%$.

<Check id="C2" />

Leverage makes the issue sharper. Let the risk-free rate be $r$. An investor puts $L$ times his wealth into a risky asset with expected return $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$, borrowing the rest at $r$ (when $L < 1$, lending the rest at $r$). The portfolio's expected return and volatility are

$$
\mu_L = r + L(\mu - r), \qquad \sigma_L = L\sigma
$$

The expected return rises in a straight line with $L$, but the volatility drag $\frac{\sigma_L^2}{2}$ grows with the square of $L$. Does more leverage always raise long-run growth? <Ref fig="2-1" /> plots the two lines.

## Volatility and annualizing [#volatility]

The standard deviation of returns (<Ref concept="standard-deviation" />) is also called volatility. A standard deviation computed from monthly or daily returns must be converted to a yearly figure to be compared. If returns in different periods are independent, the variance of the sum of $T$ periods' returns is $T$ times the variance of one period (<Ref lesson="0.3" />), so

$$
\sigma_a = \sigma_m \sqrt{12}
$$

where $\sigma_m$ is the standard deviation of monthly returns and $\sigma_a$ that of yearly returns. This gives the <Def id="annualized-volatility">annualized volatility</Def>. With daily returns, multiply by $\sqrt{252}$ (about 252 trading days a year). For example, a monthly volatility of 5% gives an annualized volatility of about $5\% \times \sqrt{12} \approx 17.3\%$.

This conversion relies on the assumption that periods are independent. In practice returns in different periods may be correlated, and then the converted figure is biased.

## Maximum drawdown [#drawdown]

The standard deviation treats rises and falls alike, and does not show how long losses last. What investors feel more directly is how far wealth falls from a high point. The largest fall in wealth from a previous peak to a later trough over a period is the <Def id="maximum-drawdown">maximum drawdown</Def>.

Two large drawdowns in US stock market history: the S&P 500 index (excluding dividends) fell about 86% from September 1929 to June 1932, and about 57% from October 2007 to March 2009. The deeper the fall, the harder it is to get back to the earlier level, and the longer it takes (<Ref check="C5" />). For investors who must withdraw money regularly (such as retirees), selling assets during a deep drawdown turns paper losses into permanent ones, so drawdowns matter more to them than the standard deviation.

## The Sharpe ratio [#sharpe]

Comparing two investments requires looking at both return and risk. Let the risk-free rate be $r_f$. The <Def id="sharpe-ratio">Sharpe ratio</Def> is the excess return earned per unit of volatility:

$$
S = \frac{\bar R - r_f}{\sigma}
$$

For example, a portfolio with an average return of 9%, a risk-free rate of 3% and a volatility of 15% has a Sharpe ratio of $\frac{9\% - 3\%}{15\%} = 0.4$.

The Sharpe ratio measures risk by the standard deviation, so it shares its limits: fat tails (<Ref concept="fat-tails" />), large losses that occur only rarely, and returns "smoothed" because an asset trades infrequently can all make it overstate the quality of an investment (<Ref check="Q3" />).

## Historical data [#history]

In data compiled by Damodaran (New York University), from 1928 to 2025 the S&P 500 (including dividends) had a geometric mean return of about 10.0% a year, 10-year US Treasury bonds about 4.5%, and 3-month Treasury bills about 3.4%. The standard deviation of yearly stock returns is about 20%, and the arithmetic mean is about 2 percentage points above the geometric mean, consistent with $\frac{\sigma^2}{2}$.

The amount by which the expected return on stocks exceeds the return on a riskless asset is the <Def id="equity-premium">equity premium</Def>. By the figures above, stocks have historically earned about 6.6 percentage points a year more than Treasury bills. Two cautions apply when using this to estimate the future equity premium:

- The estimation error is large. With a yearly standard deviation of about 20%, even nearly a century of data leaves a standard error (<Ref concept="standard-error" />) of about 2 percentage points on the average return (<Ref check="Q4" />).
- The US was one of the best-performing stock markets of the 20th century. Looking only at a market that turned out to be successful overstates the typical return on stocks.

<Ref lesson="4.11" /> discusses how to estimate future expected returns more systematically.

## Summary [#summary]

- The arithmetic mean $\bar R$ estimates next year's expected return; the geometric mean $G$ is the compound growth the holder actually got. With volatile returns, $G < \bar R$.
- Volatility drag: $G \approx \mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}$. Leverage raises the expected return in a straight line and the volatility drag with the square.
- With independent periods, annualized volatility is $\sigma_a = \sigma_m \sqrt{12}$.
- Maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a previous peak to a later trough; it shows risk that the standard deviation does not.
- The Sharpe ratio $S = \frac{\bar R - r_f}{\sigma}$ measures excess return per unit of volatility, but inherits the limits of the standard deviation.
- From 1928 to 2025, US stocks returned about 10.0% a year (geometric) and Treasury bills about 3.4%; estimates of the historical equity premium have large errors.
