A household's goals are about buying things in the future: a home, education, life in retirement. Their prices rise with inflation, so planning should use real returns (<Ref concept="real-interest-rate" />), not nominal ones.

## Planning in real terms [#real-terms]

People tend to think in nominal amounts: a 3% raise feels like getting richer even if prices also rose 3%. This tendency is called <Def id="money-illusion">money illusion</Def>. The way to avoid it in household planning is to state every goal in today's purchasing power, use real rates of return after inflation, and read the results in today's purchasing power too. Capital market assumptions (<Ref lesson="4.11" />) are usually given as real returns.

Planning must also separate incomes and expenses that adjust with inflation from those that do not: Social Security adjusts for inflation; most private pensions and ordinary annuities are fixed nominal amounts; and the payment on a fixed-rate mortgage is a fixed nominal amount too.

## Which assets protect against inflation [#hedges]

Assets protect against inflation in different ways, and one must distinguish expected from unexpected inflation, and the short run from the long run:

- Cash and Treasury bills: their rates adjust to expected inflation and keep up in the short run, but their real return is often near 0.
- Nominal bonds: unexpected inflation lowers their real value, more so the longer the maturity (<Ref lesson="3.4" />).
- Treasury inflation-protected securities (<Ref concept="tips" />): the principal adjusts with the CPI, guaranteeing a real return if held to maturity (<Ref lesson="3.6" sec="tips" />).
- The <Def id="i-bond">I bond</Def> (Series I savings bond): a savings bond issued by the US Treasury whose rate combines a fixed rate and an inflation rate reset every six months from the CPI. I bonds do not trade in a market; they can be redeemed at their accrued value, so their nominal value never falls; they must be held at least 1 year, and redeeming within 5 years forfeits the last 3 months of interest; each person can buy at most 10,000 dollars a year; interest is taxed federally only at redemption and is exempt from state tax. I bonds bought from November 2026 to April 2027 have a fixed rate of 1.40% and a composite rate of 5.51%.
- Stocks: companies' revenues and assets rise with prices, so stocks keep up with inflation in the long run, but in the short run unexpected inflation often comes with rising rates and falling valuations (<Ref lesson="2.8" sec="stock-bond" />).
- Commodities and real estate: somewhat positively related to inflation (<Ref lesson="5.5" />), but very volatile themselves.
- Fixed-rate liabilities: inflation lightens their real burden, a hedge for the borrower (<Ref lesson="6.6" sec="debt" />).

## Matching retirement spending with TIPS [#retirement-income]

Most spending in retirement rises with prices; it is a real liability. Matching it with real assets carries the least risk: for example a ladder of TIPS with different maturities, one maturing each year to provide that year's real spending (<Ref check="Q2" />). This is the same idea as immunization, matching liabilities by duration (<Ref lesson="3.4" sec="immunization" />), with a real liability in place of a nominal one. <Ref lesson="6.12" /> extends this idea to all of a household's goals.

## Personal inflation [#personal-inflation]

The CPI measures the average price change of a basket of goods and services bought by urban consumers (<Ref concept="cpi" />), but every household spends differently. Retirees spend more on health care and housing, whose prices often rise faster than average; households with mortgages are affected differently by rising home prices than renters are. The Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes an experimental index weighted by the spending of households aged 62 and over (CPI-E). Social Security is adjusted by the CPI-W; the 2026 adjustment was 2.8%.

## Summary [#summary]

- Plan in today's purchasing power and real returns, avoiding money illusion; separate incomes and expenses that adjust with inflation from those that do not.
- Nominal bonds suffer from unexpected inflation; TIPS and I bonds protect real value; stocks keep up with inflation in the long run but not necessarily in the short run; fixed-rate liabilities favor the borrower.
- Retirement spending is a real liability that a TIPS ladder can match.
- Personal inflation can differ from the CPI, especially for retirees.
