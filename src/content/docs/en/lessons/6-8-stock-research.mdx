Earlier lessons showed that for most households low-cost index funds are the core of the portfolio (<Ref lesson="6.3" />). This lesson briefly introduces the process of researching a single stock: even without picking stocks, these methods help in understanding the companies one owns, judging other people's recommendations, and writing the stock report in the capstone project.

## Reading company filings [#filings]

All filings of US listed companies are freely available on the SEC's EDGAR system:

- The <Def id="form-10-k">Form 10-K</Def> is the audited annual report, filed by large companies within 60 days of the fiscal year end. The parts most worth reading are: the business description (how the company makes money, its segments and customers), risk factors, management's discussion and analysis (MD&A, which explains why results changed), and the financial statements and notes (<Ref lesson="3.7" />).
- The 10-Q is the quarterly report: unaudited, similar to the 10-K but shorter.
- The 8-K is filed within 4 business days of a major event (mergers, management changes, earnings releases and so on).
- The proxy statement (DEF 14A) shows the structure and targets of executive pay and the makeup of the board.
- On the earnings call, management presents results and answers analysts' questions, often giving guidance for the next quarter or year.

## Industry analysis [#industry]

A company's long-run profitability depends heavily on its industry. Porter's (1979) <Def id="five-forces">five forces</Def> analyze how much of an industry's profit stays with the companies in it: the intensity of rivalry among existing firms; the threat of new entrants (<Ref concept="barriers-to-entry" />); the threat of substitutes; the bargaining power of suppliers; and the bargaining power of buyers. The weaker the five forces, the higher the industry's average profits.

Within an industry, whether a company can earn more than its cost of capital for a long time depends on whether it has a moat (<Ref concept="economic-moat" />): network effects, switching costs, economies of scale, brands or patents. The measure is the gap between return on invested capital (<Ref concept="return-on-invested-capital" />) and the weighted average cost of capital (<Ref concept="wacc" />), and how long that gap can last (<Ref check="Q3" />).

## Valuation: running the DCF in reverse [#valuation]

DCF (<Ref lesson="3.10" />) and relative valuation (<Ref lesson="3.11" />) both depend on assumptions about the future, and the results are very sensitive to growth and discount rates. A practical approach turns the question around: instead of estimating what the company is worth, ask "what growth does today's price imply the market expects?"; this is a <Def id="reverse-dcf">reverse DCF</Def>. Then judge whether that growth is reasonable: compare it with the company's history, the size of its industry and the past experience of similar companies.

## The thesis and the case against it [#thesis]

The result of research should be written up as an <Def id="investment-thesis">investment thesis</Def>: why the price is below value now, how one's view differs from the market consensus, what events might make the market see it, and what evidence would prove one wrong.

Just as important is writing out the case against seriously: what are the strongest reasons to be bearish? One can do a "pre-mortem": suppose that three years from now this investment has lost 50%; what is the most likely reason? The aim is to counter confirmation bias and overconfidence (<Ref concept="overconfidence" />): people tend to look for information that supports their views.

Finally there is position size. Even after thorough research, a single stock's idiosyncratic risk is large (<Ref lesson="4.5" />), and the judgment of value may be wrong. A common practice is to limit any one stock's share of the portfolio, keeping individual stocks as a small part outside the index core.

## Summary [#summary]

- 10-Ks, 10-Qs, 8-Ks, proxy statements and earnings calls are the basic sources for researching a company; the 10-K is audited.
- The five forces analyze how much of an industry's profit companies keep; a moat decides whether ROIC can stay above WACC for long.
- A reverse DCF finds the growth implied by the price, which can then be judged for reasonableness.
- An investment thesis should state how one differs from the market and what evidence would prove one wrong; write the case against seriously; limit the size of single-stock positions.
