Stage 4 covered the theory of portfolios, capital market assumptions and lifecycle allocation. This lesson turns them into an allocation plan a household can carry out.

## Strategic and tactical allocation [#strategic-tactical]

<Def id="strategic-asset-allocation">Strategic asset allocation</Def> is the long-term target mix, for example 60% stocks and 40% bonds. It comes from three things: the household's goals and horizon; the lower of its risk capacity (how large a loss it can bear financially, which depends on human capital, the emergency fund, debts and so on, <Ref lesson="6.6" />) and its risk tolerance (how much fluctuation it can bear psychologically); and estimates of the long-run returns and risks of each asset class (<Ref lesson="4.11" />). The strategic allocation is written into the investment policy statement (IPS) and does not change with short-term market moves.

<Def id="tactical-asset-allocation">Tactical asset allocation</Def> is a temporary departure from the strategic mix based on a view of the short-term market, for example cutting stocks when they look expensive. It is in effect market timing, and like other active management it faces the evidence on market efficiency (<Ref lesson="4.7" />) and costs (<Ref lesson="6.3" />).

## Lifecycle and glide paths [#glide-path]

<Ref lesson="4.12" /> showed that if human capital is bond-like, a young person's financial assets can hold more stocks, declining with age. The glide path of a target-date fund (<Ref concept="target-date-fund" />) standardizes this idea: for example about 90% stocks 40 years before retirement, falling to 40%–50% at retirement. Some funds reach their lowest stock share at the retirement date ("to"), others keep reducing it for some years after retirement ("through").

A particular household's path can depart from the standard: people with stable incomes or pensions can hold more stocks; people whose income moves closely with the stock market (for example in finance or technology, with lots of employer stock) should hold fewer; and large falls in the years just before and after retirement matter most, as <Ref lesson="6.9" /> discusses.

## International diversification [#international]

By market value, US stocks make up more than 60% of the global stock market (MSCI ACWI Index, 2025), yet US investors often keep most or even all of their stocks in the US; this is called <Def id="home-bias">home bias</Def>. The market portfolio of theory (<Ref concept="market-portfolio" />) is global.

Correlations between national stock markets have been fairly high since 2000, so international diversification does little to reduce short-term volatility. Its value shows more over the long run: a single country can go through very long slumps; for example the Nikkei 225's peak of December 1989 was not surpassed until February 2024. Holding foreign assets also brings exchange-rate risk (<Ref lesson="2.7" sec="fx-risk" />): for stocks, currency moves are a small part of total volatility and are usually left unhedged; for bonds, currency moves can be larger than the bonds' own volatility and are usually hedged (<Ref concept="currency-hedging" />). Some common target-date funds hold about 40% of their stocks outside the US.

## Rebalancing [#rebalancing]

Because assets earn different returns, a portfolio drifts away from its targets. <Def id="rebalancing">Rebalancing</Def> means selling assets whose weight is too high and buying those whose weight is too low, bringing the portfolio back to its targets. There are two common rules: rebalance on a calendar (for example once a year), or rebalance when a weight strays more than a set amount from its target (a band rule, for example ±5 percentage points).

Rebalancing has costs too: trading costs, and in taxable accounts capital gains tax on selling appreciated assets (<Ref lesson="6.5" />). In practice these can be reduced: use new contributions, dividends and interest to buy the assets that are underweight; make the adjustments in tax-deferred accounts; trade only when the drift is large.

## Summary [#summary]

- The strategic allocation is the long-term target mix, set by goals, the lower of risk capacity and tolerance, and capital market assumptions; tactical allocation is short-term timing.
- Glide paths reduce the stock share with age; a particular household should adjust for the nature of its human capital.
- The US is more than 60% of the global stock market; the benefit of international diversification shows mainly over the long run; foreign stocks are usually unhedged and foreign bonds usually hedged.
- Rebalancing brings the portfolio back to its targets, on a calendar or by bands; cash flows and tax-deferred accounts reduce its costs.
